The Case for Taking the Long Way Home

We stopped using the interstate for anything under four hours. Here is what changed, and why we are not going back.

By the Two Lane Journal editors · Updated October 2026

For years our trips home followed the same script. Merge, set the cruise control, stare at the back of a trailer for three hours, arrive tired. The route was efficient. It was also forgettable.

Last spring a closure pushed us onto a state highway we had never driven. It wound through two small towns, past a feed store with a hand-painted sign and a diner that still served pie by the slice. We got home thirty minutes later than planned, and we talked about that drive for a week.

That was the start of a simple rule: if the trip is under four hours, we take the slow road. Here is what we have learned since.

The time cost is smaller than you think

We logged twelve trips over six months and compared the two routes. On average the back roads added eighteen minutes to a three-hour drive. On two trips they were actually faster, because we skipped construction and a holiday backup.

"Eighteen minutes buys you a view, a decent sandwich and the feeling that you went somewhere. That is a good trade."

You start noticing things

On the interstate the landscape is a blur between exits. On a two-lane road you see the river you have been crossing for years, the old rail bridge, the orchard that sells cider in October. The trip stops being dead time and becomes part of the weekend.

How we plan it

  • Pick one anchor stop. A lookout, a bakery, a bookstore. One is enough. More than two and the drive turns into a schedule.
  • Leave on time. The slow road is only fun when nobody is watching the clock. We leave thirty minutes earlier than we think we need to.
  • Keep a paper map in the glovebox. Coverage drops in the hills. A folded map never does.
  • Fill the tank before you turn off. Stations get sparse. Starting full means no math on the way.

Try it once on a drive you know well. Pick the road that runs parallel to the highway and see where it goes. The worst case is twenty extra minutes. The best case is a new favorite route.

A Weekend on the Parkway, Done on a Budget

We get asked about this drive more than any other, so here is the short version. We left Friday after lunch, stayed two nights in a small town just off the route, and drove the middle section both directions so we could catch the overlooks in morning and evening light.

  • Friday: three hours of driving, two overlooks, dinner at the only place open past eight. Worth it.
  • Saturday: the long day. Six overlooks, a short walk to a waterfall, a picnic from the grocery store in town. Fog in the morning, clear by ten.
  • Sunday: one last stop for coffee at the overlook with the stone wall, then the slow road home.

What we would skip next time: the visitor center gift shop and the second waterfall, which was a long walk for a short view. What we would repeat: the early start on Saturday. The first hour of light on those ridges is the whole reason to go.

Guides

What is actually in our glovebox

We cleaned it out this fall and were surprised how little we use. What stayed: a paper road atlas, a tire gauge, a small flashlight, napkins, a phone cable, and a pen that works. What went: three expired coupons, a cassette adapter and a stack of receipts from 2019.

Finding the good diner

Our rule is simple. Look for the parking lot with local plates and work trucks at seven in the morning. Skip anything with a billboard. Order whatever the person at the counter is eating.

The Two-Bag Rule for a Five-Day Trip

One canvas duffel goes in the trunk and does not come out until the hotel. One small daypack rides up front with everything we need on the road: map, thermos, sunglasses, a layer for the overlooks, snacks, chargers. If it does not fit in those two bags, it stays home.

The rule sounds strict. In practice it ends the thirty-minute scramble before every trip and the pile of unused things in the back seat after it. Five days, two bags, no regrets so far.
